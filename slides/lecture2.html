Module 1.2
The Power Wall

By the end you can…

  • Distinguish energy from power, and define EPI, EDP, ED²P
  • Decompose power into dynamic (CV²f) and static (leakage)
  • Explain why voltage stopped scaling and frequency stalled ~2005

Basics of Power & Energy

\(V\) voltage\(I\) current\(C\) capacitance\(f\) switching frequency (clock)
EnergyAbility to do work — unit joule.
  • 1 J ≈ lift an apple to shoulder height
  • 4 J ≈ heat a teaspoon of water by 1 °C
PowerRate of energy transfer — 1 watt = 1 J/s.   \(P = V \cdot I\)

For a capacitor: \(E_{stored} = \tfrac{1}{2} C V^2\)

Charged/drained f times per second:

\( P_{dynamic} = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 C V^2 \cdot f = C V^2 f \)

This dynamic power term drives everything in this module.

Efficiency Metrics

IPS = instructions / secondFLOPS = floating-point operations / second

Energy efficiency

  • Energy per work — e.g. EPI (energy/instruction)
  • Power per throughput — e.g. Watt/IPS, Watt/FLOPS
\( \frac{Watt}{thruput}=\frac{J/s}{work/s}=\frac{J}{work} \)

= amount of energy required to perform the work

\( \frac{W}{IPS}=\frac{J/s}{inst/s}=\frac{J}{inst}=EPI \)

Weighting performance in

  • EDP (Energy-Delay Product) = Energy × Delay
  • ED²P (Energy-Delay² Product) = Energy × Delay² — emphasizes speed even more
Why?Two systems both use 1 J: A = 0.5 W for 2 s, B = 1 W for 1 s. Most prefer B — so we weight energy by delay.

Power Consumption

\(A\) fraction of gates switching\(C\) capacitance\(V\) supply voltage\(f\) frequency\(I_{leak}\) leakage current

Total power is capped by packaging (e.g. ~65–150 W desktop). It has two parts:

\( P_{consumption} = P_{dynamic} + P_{static} \)
Dynamic powerCharging/discharging capacitive loads
\( P_{dynamic} = A\,C\,V^2 f \)
Static powerLeakage even when idle
\( P_{static} = V \cdot I_{leak} \)
\(I_{leak}\) grows fast as transistors shrink — the modern villain.
Traditionally (older / larger process, while the chip is active), dynamic power dominates.

Max Frequency vs. Threshold Voltage

V_thd = threshold voltage — minimum voltage to make a transistor conductive
\( f_{max} \approx c\,\dfrac{(V - V_{thd})^{1.3}}{V} \)
Lower V_thdimproves f_max (good for performance).
TakeawayReducing threshold voltage increases maximum frequency almost linearly.

Static Power: Leakage Current

V_thd threshold voltageq electron chargek Boltzmann constantT temperature
\( I_{leak} > e^{-\frac{q\,V_{thd}}{kT}} \)
Lower V_thd↑ leakage current exponentially → static power blows up.
TakeawayReducing threshold voltage increases static power consumption exponentially.
As the process shrinks (nanometer nodes) — and whenever the chip is idle — static power grows increasingly important, even overtaking dynamic.

Threshold Voltage Dilemma

Decreasing V_thdimproves f_max (and hence performance) — almost linearly.
\( f_{max} \approx c\,\dfrac{(V - V_{thd})^{1.3}}{V} \)
Decreasing V_thdincreases leakage current (hence static power) — exponentially.
\( I_{leak} > e^{-\frac{q\,V_{thd}}{kT}} \)
The dilemmaYou cannot keep lowering V_thd to chase speed — leakage punishes you.

Old Rule: Dennard Scaling

Per generation, gate length scales by S = 0.7.

Scaling per generation
  • Capacitance (C) scales by S = 0.7
  • Area scales by S² = 0.5 → number of transistors scales by 1/S² = 2×
  • Supply voltage (V) scales by S = 0.7
  • Frequency (f) scales by 1/S = 1.4
\(P_{dynamic} = ACV^2f \Rightarrow\) chip power constant 🎉
Why is chip power constant?

Per device, substitute \(C\!\to\!SC,\ V\!\to\!SV,\ f\!\to\!f/S\):

\( A(SC)(SV)^2\tfrac{f}{S} = ACV^2 f\cdot S^2 \)

Power per device \(\times S^2\), but transistors/chip \(\times 1/S^2\) ⇒ \(S^2\cdot\tfrac{1}{S^2}=1\) — constant for any S.

Named after R. H. Dennard et al., “Design of Ion-Implanted MOSFET’s with Very Small Physical Dimensions,” IEEE J. Solid-State Circuits, 1974 (a.k.a. classical / constant-field scaling).

New Rule: Leakage-Limited

Same S = 0.7, but supply voltage V can no longer scale down (lowering it needs lower V_thd → leakage explodes).

Scaling per generation
  • Capacitance (C) scales by S = 0.7
  • Number of transistors scales by 1/S² = 2×
  • Supply voltage (V) cannot scale (V_thd → leakage)
  • Frequency (f) scales by 1/S = 1.4
Why power doubles

Now V is fixed:

\( A(SC)(V)^2\tfrac{f}{S} = ACV^2 f \)

Per-device power unchanged; transistors/chip \(\times 1/S^2\):

\( 1\times\tfrac{1}{S^2} = \tfrac{1}{S^2}\approx 2 \Rightarrow\) power doubles / generation! 🔥

The Power Wall

Power WallDennard scaling ended (~2005). More transistors no longer come "for free" in power — so we can't just crank frequency anymore.

The fix: stop raising frequency, spend the extra transistors on more cores — and push performance onto software & architecture.

Implications for Architecture Design

FrequencyCranking the clock has hit a dead end.
ResourcesArea is abundant, power is scarce — the inversion.
Per-core budgetDeclining power budget per core.
New objective function Architectures must improve energy efficiency. The dominant metric becomes performance / watt (ops / joule) — and the answer is many simpler, cooler cores: multicore.

Evidence: Frequency Went Flat (~2001–2005)

source: CPU DB (real per-chip data) · red = trend

For decades clock frequency climbed every generation. Then, around 2001–2005, it flattened — and has stayed on a plateau ever since.

The symptomThe first visible sign of the power wall: clock frequency stopped climbing.

Evidence: Pipelining Reversed

Logic per pipeline stage (FO4 per cycle) fell through the 1990s as designers super-pipelined to chase frequency.

After ~2005The trend reversed — stages grew longer again. Deep pipelines cost too much power for too little gain.
source: CPU DB · FO4 = fan-out-of-4 gate delay (a process-independent unit of logic)

Evidence: Power Hit the Ceiling

Projected power kept rising exponentially; actual shipped power flattened around 2004–2005.

The ceilingDesigners capped power at the packaging / cooling limit instead of following the projection — the power wall, in data.
source: CPU DB

Three Signatures of the Power Wall

All three appear in the same 2001–2005 window — the same window as the multicore transition:

1 · FrequencyClock frequency stops climbing.
2 · PipeliningLogic per pipeline stage stops shrinking (pipelining reverses).
3 · Power mgmtPower-saving techniques (clock gating, DVFS, power gating) become essential.

Not a Coincidence

Same event, two anglesThe power wall and the multicore pivot are the same event seen from two angles.

Once frequency could no longer rise, the only way to keep using the extra transistors Moore's law still delivered was to add more cores — pushing the burden of performance onto software & architecture.

In-Class Exercises — Q1

Q1If Moore's law slows so gate length scales at S = 0.8 (instead of 0.7), find dynamic power consumption for the next generation under unlimited vs. limited voltage scaling.
recall   \( P_{dynamic} = A\,C\,V^2 f \)
Old / unlimited scaling C↓0.8 · area↓0.64 → 1.56× transistors · V↓0.8 · f↑1.25
\(P' = 1.56 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 0.8^2 \cdot 1.25 \approx 1.0\) → constant
New / leakage-limited V fixed, f↑1.25, 1.56× transistors
\(P' = 1.56 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 1.25 \approx 1.56\) → +56% / gen

In-Class Exercises — Q2

Q2With limited voltage scaling, we want to keep dynamic power consumption constant in the next generation by keeping frequency constant and reducing die area. How much should we reduce die area?

Answer (S = 0.7, V & f fixed)

  • Per device: only C scales — \(C\to SC=0.7C\). So one transistor's power \(ACV^2f \to 0.7\times\).
  • Same die area: transistors/chip \(\times 1/S^2 = 2\times\). Chip power \(= 2\times0.7 = \mathbf{1.4\times}\) (too high).
  • Shrink area by factor \(a\) (transistors ∝ area): power \(=(2a)(0.7)=1.4a\). Set \(=1\):
\( 1.4\,a = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; a = \dfrac{1}{1.4} \approx 0.71 \)

→ keep die area at ~71% of the old size, i.e. reduce area by ≈ 29%.

Module 1.2 — Review

1. Difference between energy and power?

Power is the rate of energy consumption (J/s = W).

2. Impact of threshold-voltage choice on static vs. dynamic power as transistors shrink?

Lowering V_thd lets you lower V → dynamic power drops (≈linearly), but static/leakage power rises (exponentially).

3. How has processor design adapted to the power wall?

Stalled frequency growth, went multicore, and added power management: clock gating, V/f scaling (DVFS), power gating.
Module 1.3
Parallel Computers

By the end you can…

  • Unpack the definition of a parallel computer (communicate + cooperate)
  • Explain why parallel machines were niche until multicore
  • Classify any system with Flynn's taxonomy: SISD / SIMD / MISD / MIMD

What Is a Parallel Architecture?

"A parallel computer is a collection of processing elements that can communicate and cooperate to solve a large problem fast."
— Almasi & Gottlieb

Three design axes hide in that sentence — we unpack each next.

☺ asideQ: Why did the parallel computers refuse to work together?
A: Too many "threads" of disagreement.

Unpacking the Definition

Collection of PEs What is a PE? How many? How powerful each?
Few powerful (Altix) vs. many small (BlueGene).
Communicate Shared memory vs. message passing. Interconnect: bus / crossbar / multistage. Sets cost, latency, throughput, scalability, fault tolerance.
Cooperate Synchronization sequences operations for correctness. Granularity & mechanism affect latency, load balance, fairness.
"…solve a large problem fast" General- vs. special-purpose: any machine solves some problems well. These axes — PEs, communication, cooperation — recur for the rest of the course.

Where Are Parallel Computers Used?

Embarrassingly parallelMany scientific codes — scale easily.
Medium parallelEngineering apps: finite-elements, VLSI-CAD.
Not parallelCompilers, editors — limited parallelism.
Modern realityAI/ML training & inference, data analytics, and graphics are now the dominant embarrassingly-parallel workloads driving GPUs and accelerators.

Why Parallel — and Why Not Until Multicore?

Why parallel?

  • Absolute performance: protein folding = years on one CPU, days on a parallel machine; weather forecasts must be timely
  • Cost-/power-adjusted performance, esp. small 2–8 chip systems — the smallest of which is a multicore

Why niche before ~2005?

  • Low-hanging ILP made single-thread perf scale cheaply
  • Parallel systems were costly & slow to design
  • ILP gains eclipsed parallel gains within a few years

Illustration: ILP Catches Up

100-processor system, perfect speedup, vs. one improving single processor:

  • Yr 1: 100× → Yr 2: 62.5× → Yr 3: 39× → … → Yr 10: 0.9×
Even worseMultiprocessors take longer to build · low volume → high prices · high prices delay adoption · perfect speedup is unattainable.

Flynn's Taxonomy [1972]

Classify machines by two independent dimensions:

Instruction streamInstructions fetched from a single program counter.
Data streamData items accessed by a single instruction.
Single DataMultiple Data
Single InstructionSISD — classic uniprocessorSIMD — vector, GPU
Multiple InstructionMISD — systolic (rare)MIMD — multicore, clusters

What's an "Instruction Stream"?

One PC points to one instruction; executed logically one at a time; PC advances.

C source

void MyFunction() {
  int a, b, c;
  a = 10; b = 5; c = 2;
}
→ one instruction stream (x86)

push ebp
mov  ebp, esp
sub  esp, 12
mov  [ebp-4], 10
mov  [ebp-8], 5
mov  [ebp-12], 2

SISD & SIMD

SISD

One PC; each instruction touches one data set. The classic uniprocessor / a single core today.

SIMD

One instruction, many data lanes. Exploits data parallelism. Examples: vector processors, multimedia extensions (SSE), GPUs.

SIMD in Code: SSE

SISD style — scalar loop

// C = A + B, element by element
for (i = 0; i < 4; i++)
    C[i] = A[i] + B[i];

Four separate add instructions.

SIMD style — 4×32-bit in a 128-bit reg

MOVUPS XMM0, [EAX]   ; load A[0..3]
MOVUPS XMM1, [EBX]   ; load B[0..3]
ADDPS  XMM0, XMM1    ; 4 adds at once
MOVUPS [Ret], XMM0   ; store C[0..3]

ADDPS = one instruction, four parallel adds.

MISD & Systolic Arrays

MISD = multiple instruction streams, single data stream. The rarest class.

  • Systolic arrays [Kung & Leiserson '79] — data "pulses" through a grid of PEs, each applying its own operation
  • Examples: space-shuttle computers (1984), Intel iWARP (1990)
ComebackThe systolic idea is alive in modern AI accelerators — Google's TPU matrix unit is a systolic array.

MIMD & Its Variants

MIMD = independent PEs, each with its own instruction and data stream. Most CPU parallel computers today

Variants differ by which level of the memory hierarchy is shared:

  • Shared main memory (SMP / multicore)
  • Shared cache (cores on one die)
  • Nothing shared (clusters / distributed memory)

The Taxonomy Today

SISDLives inside a single core — handles ILP.
SIMDThrives in GPUs & TPUs — data parallelism for HPC, blockchain, AI.
MIMDMulticore CPUs — TLP & complex parallelism.
MISDStill a niche — but systolic arrays power AI matrix engines.
Real systems combine all of them: a cloud/HPC server is SISD cores, SIMD GPUs, in a MIMD fabric.

SISD, SIMD, MISD, MIMD.

2. Classify each system:

  • GPU / Xeon-Phi-style co-processor
  • A multicore chip in a server

Performance Metrics

By the end you can…

  • Define execution time and total parallel overhead
  • Compute speedup (vs. the best sequential algorithm) and efficiency

The Four Metrics

Before optimizing, define the way you will measure — in advance.

We will define
  • Execution time
  • Speedup
  • Efficiency
  • Cost
MetricDefinition
Execution time\(T_P\) (parallel), \(T_S\) (serial)
Speedup\(S = T_S / T_P\)
Efficiency\(E = S/p\)
Cost\(C = p\,T_P\)

All four are connected — we derive the links as we go.

Execution Time

Definition Elapsed (wall-clock) time from start to when the last PE finishes.
  • \(T_P\) = parallel execution time, \(T_S\) = serial execution time
  • Used both for performance and for usage charging

Typical charging policy (e.g. on a shared cluster):

SUs = #processors allocated × wall time × priority
Associated metric Total parallel overhead
\( T_o \;=\; p\,T_P \;-\; T_S \)
All the extra processor–time spent that the serial run never paid.

Note the "last PE finishes" rule: one straggler sets the whole wall-clock time — which is exactly why load imbalance is so costly.

Speedup

Definition
\( S \;=\; \dfrac{T_S}{T_P} \)
How many times faster the parallel run is than the serial run.
Read the fine print \(T_S\) is the execution time of the BEST sequential algorithm — not a crippled serial version of the parallel code.
Super-linear speedup (S > p) Possible! From a hardware advantage the serial run never had:
  • Better use of faster storage — the per-PE working set now fits in cache / memory
  • Exploratory decomposition — a parallel search finds the answer early

Efficiency

Definition The fraction of time a PE is usefully employed.
\( E \;=\; \dfrac{S}{p} \)

It is the ratio between actual and ideal performance:

  • \(E = 1\) (= 100%) → perfect linear speedup
  • \(E < 1\) → some PE time is wasted on overhead / idling
Why efficiency, not just speedup Speedup of 20 sounds great — but on 40 PEs that is \(E = 0.5\): half your machine is wasted. Efficiency normalizes for the resources you spent.

Efficiency is the metric we hold constant when we talk about scalability (Module C).

Cost

Definition Resource-usage cost, charged as the processor–time product:
\( C \;=\; p\,T_P \)
Load imbalance hurts twice Idle time is still charged — a PE waiting on a straggler costs the same as a PE working.

Watch where scaling stops. Example — diminishing returns:

pSpeedupEfficiency
16140.88
32200.63

Relationship between efficiency and cost?

\(E = \dfrac{S}{p} = \dfrac{T_S/T_P}{p} = \dfrac{T_S}{p\,T_P} = \dfrac{T_S}{C}\) — efficiency is just serial time over cost.

Module B — Review

1. Against which serial time is speedup measured, and why does it matter?

Against the best sequential algorithm's \(T_S\). Otherwise you could fake a big speedup by comparing against a bad serial baseline.

2. A program gets speedup 20 on 32 PEs. What is its efficiency?

\(E = S/p = 20/32 = 0.625\) — about 63% of the machine is doing useful work.

3. Express efficiency in terms of cost.

\(E = T_S / C\), where \(C = p\,T_P\).

Scalability

By the end you can…

  • Define scalability and problem size W
  • Derive \(T_P\), speedup, and efficiency for the summing example
  • State isoefficiency and use it to judge whether a system scales

What Is Scalability?

Parallel programs must accommodate growth in two dimensions:

  • The problem size
  • The number of PEs
Scalability The capacity to increase speedup in proportion to the number of PEs.
Problem size W The amount of computation — the basic steps of the best sequential algorithm, not just the data size. Correlated with serial execution time.
Scalable parallel system One that can maintain efficiency by simultaneously increasing #PEs and problem size.

Example: Summing n Numbers on p PEs

Decompose: spread the n numbers evenly across PE 0 … PE p−1.

  • Each PE owns a contiguous block of n / p numbers
  • This is a simple data decomposition
Two phases ahead Each PE first sums its own block (a local sum), then the PEs merge the local sums into one global sum.

Summing: The Algorithm

  • Local sum: each PE adds its n/p numbers → cost n / p
  • Tree merge: combine local sums pairwise up a binary tree → cost 2 log p
Parallel time
\( T_P \;=\; \dfrac{n}{p} \;+\; 2\log p \)
First term shrinks with p; second term grows with p — that tension is the whole scalability story.

Summing: Speedup

Speedup
\( S \;=\; \dfrac{n}{T_P} \;=\; \dfrac{np}{\,n + 2p\log p\,} \)
  • Larger n stays closer to linear for longer
  • Small n = 64 plateaus near 5 — overhead dominates quickly
Lesson For a fixed problem, more PEs eventually stop helping. To keep scaling, you must also grow n.

Summing: Efficiency

Efficiency
\( E \;=\; \dfrac{S}{p} \;=\; \dfrac{n}{\,n + 2p\log p\,} \)
  • All curves decline as p grows (overhead 2p log p in the denominator)
  • Bigger n stays near 1.0 longer
The fix To hold efficiency constant, grow n as you grow p. How much? → isoefficiency.

Isoefficiency

Write efficiency in terms of problem size W, #PEs p, and the overhead function T(W,p):

\( E \;=\; \dfrac{1}{\,1 + T(W,p)/W\,} \)

For a scalable system, hold E constant. Solving for W:

\( W \;=\; \dfrac{E}{1-E}\,T(W,p) \;=\; K\cdot T(W,p) \)

(K is a constant set by the target efficiency.)

The isoefficiency function W(p) How much you must grow the problem to keep the same efficiency as you add PEs.
  • W(p) ≤ O(p) scalable (grows at most linearly)
  • W(p) = O(p²) not scalable — problem must explode
Summing example \(T(W,p) = 2p\log p \Rightarrow W = K\cdot 2p\log p\) — grows a bit faster than linear → somewhat scalable.

Module C — Review

1. What is "problem size W" — is it the data size?

No — W is the amount of computation: the basic steps of the best sequential algorithm. It correlates with serial execution time.

2. For summing, why does efficiency fall as p grows at fixed n?

\(E = n/(n + 2p\log p)\). The overhead term \(2p\log p\) grows with p while n is fixed, so the denominator grows and E drops.

3. State the isoefficiency relation and what W(p) ≤ O(p) means.

\(W = K\,T(W,p)\). If W only needs to grow linearly (or less) with p to hold efficiency, the system is scalable.
Module D
Reducing Parallel Overhead

By the end you can…

  • Parallelize matrix multiply with a simple dynamic scheme
  • Model communication with α (per-message) and β (per-byte)
  • Compute a computation–communication ratio and improve it by blocking

Case Study: Matrix Multiply

How would we parallelize this? Start with the serial triple-nested loop:

Serial matmul — C = A × B

for (i = 0; i < n; i++)
  for (j = 0; j < n; j++) {
    C[i][j] = 0;
    for (k = 0; k < n; k++)
      C[i][j] += A[i][k] * B[k][j];
  }

Each element C[i][j] needs row i of A and column j of B.

The questions
  • How do we distribute A and B?
  • How large is the overhead?
  • How much does a message / remote access cost?

A Simple Dynamic Algorithm

  • Distribute A by rows, B by columns
  • Any processor can request a row of A or a column of B (two messages)
  • Distribute the work of computing each element of C via a load-balancing scheme
Bonus Dynamic load balancing works even on heterogeneous machines — fast PEs naturally grab more work.
But how much does it cost? To answer "how large is the overhead?" we need a model of communication, then a computation–communication ratio:
\( \dfrac{\text{useful compute}}{\text{communication}} \)
High ratio = compute-bound (good). Low ratio = communication-bound (bad).

Modeling Communication: α and β

The model Cost of sending a message of m bytes:
\( \alpha + m\,\beta \)
  • \(\alpha\) = per-message cost (latency / startup)
  • \(\beta\) = per-byte cost (bandwidth)
The key asymmetry Per-message cost \(\alpha\) is ≫ per-byte cost \(\beta\) — by a factor of several thousand.
Optimization idea Send fewer messages; combine messages. Amortize the huge fixed \(\alpha\) over more useful bytes.

Ratio: The Simple Dynamic Algorithm

Cost to compute one element of C:

  • Computation = 2n (one + and one * per k; 1 unit time each)
  • Communication = two messages of size n = \(2(\alpha + n\beta)\)
\( \text{ratio} \;=\; \dfrac{n}{\alpha + n\beta} \)
Plug in numbers \(\alpha = 1000,\ \beta = 0.2,\ n = 1000\):
\( \dfrac{1000}{1000 + 1000\cdot 0.2} = \dfrac{1000}{1200} \approx 0.83 \)
Ratio < 1 → communication-bound. We move two whole vectors just to do 2n flops. Bad.

A Better Algorithm: Blocking

Store A as row-bunches (g rows each) and B as column-bunches. Each object computes a g × g section of C.

  • Computation = \(2\,g\,g\,n\) ops
  • Communication = 2 messages, \(g\,n\) bytes each
\( \text{ratio} \;=\; \dfrac{g^2 n}{\alpha + g n\beta} \)
Plug in numbers \(\alpha = 1000,\ \beta = 0.2,\ n = 1000,\ g = 10\):
\( \dfrac{10^2\cdot 1000}{1000 + 10\cdot 1000\cdot 0.2} = \dfrac{100000}{3000} \approx 33.3 \)
From 0.83 → 33.3: a 40× better ratio. Now compute-bound.
Why it works Larger blocks → higher arithmetic intensity → the fixed message cost \(\alpha\) is amortized over far more compute.

Module D — Review

1. What do α and β represent, and which dominates?

\(\alpha\) = per-message (startup) cost, \(\beta\) = per-byte cost. \(\alpha \gg \beta\) by ~several thousand, so message count dominates.

2. Why does the simple dynamic matmul give a ratio of only 0.83?

Per element it does just 2n flops but ships two n-element messages: \(n/(\alpha + n\beta) = 1000/1200 \approx 0.83\) — communication-bound.

3. How does blocking raise the ratio to 33.3?

A g×g block does \(g^2 n\) flops per pair of messages: \(g^2 n/(\alpha + gn\beta)\). Bigger blocks amortize \(\alpha\) over more compute → higher arithmetic intensity.

Performance — Big Picture

A · LimitsSpeedup is capped by the serial fraction (Amdahl, \(S = 1/s\)) and by real overhead — so reality peaks then declines.
B · MetricsTime, speedup (vs. best sequential), efficiency \(E = S/p\), cost \(C = p\,T_P\).
C · ScalabilityKeep efficiency up as p and W grow; isoefficiency \(W = K\,T(W,p)\) quantifies it.
One sentence Reduce overhead by optimizing communication — fewer, bigger messages → better computation–communication ratio. The rest of the course (coherence, consistency, interconnect) is all about managing this overhead.
