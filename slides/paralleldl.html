Topic 6

Parallel Deep Learning — Data, Tensor, Pipeline & 3D Parallelism for Training Giant Models
This topic applies everything we learned about shared memory, message passing, collectives and interconnects to the workload that now dominates HPC clusters: training deep neural networks. The organizing question is simple — a modern model and its training state no longer fit on one accelerator, so how do we split the work across thousands of GPUs while keeping them busy? Every technique here is drawn from and cited to its primary source; the last two slides list full references with links.

Models and Data Outgrow One GPU

The size wall GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters [Brown et al. 2020]; Megatron-Turing NLG has 530 billion [Smith et al. 2022]. At mixed-precision Adam a model needs ~16 bytes per parameter of state [Rajbhandari et al. 2020] → 175B ⇒ ~2.8 TB, far beyond any single 40–80 GB GPU.
The compute wall Datasets are hundreds of billions of tokens; one pass on one device would take years. We must process many samples at once.
Adapted from parameter counts reported in [Brown et al. 2020], [Smith et al. 2022], [Fedus et al. 2021].
Consequence When neither the state nor the work fits on one device, training becomes a distributed-systems problem — the subject of this topic.

Recap: The Transformer (What We're Parallelizing)

Text is cut into tokens; each token becomes a vector, and the model is a stack of identical layers. [Vaswani et al. 2017]

tokens → embeddings [seq × d] → N × { self-attention → FFN → LayerNorm } → predict the next token
Per-token (independent) Embedding, FFN/MLP, LayerNorm, residual — every token is processed on its own, no mixing across the sequence → zero cross-GPU communication under CP.
Cross-token (the mixer) Self-attention is the only step where a token looks at all other tokens. It is why long context is expensive, and the one place CP must exchange K/V.
LLM generation An LLM is autoregressive: predict one token, append it, repeat. At inference the past tokens' K/V are stored in a KV cache to avoid recomputation.

Self-Attention: Q, K, V

Each token vector \(x\) is projected three ways by learned weight matrices — these are the layer weights that tensor parallelism splits:

\( q = x\,W_Q,\qquad k = x\,W_K,\qquad v = x\,W_V \)
Q — querywhat this token is looking for
K — keywhat a token offers to be matched on
V — valuethe content that actually gets aggregated
\( \text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\dfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \)
Why it matters here Generating Q/K/V is per-token; the \(QK^\top\) score is cross-token. Multi-head runs several Q/K/V sets at once — exactly how tensor parallelism splits attention (by head), while context parallelism splits the sequence.

Recap: Mini-Batch SGD & the Memory Budget

One training step
  1. Forward: compute loss on a mini-batch, saving activations
  2. Backward: backprop gradients \( g=\nabla_\theta L \)
  3. Update: \( \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\hat{g} \) (Adam uses momentum + variance)
Two things must be split The state (params + grads + optimizer + activations) and the work (the FLOPs of forward/backward). Different parallelism strategies target different pieces.

Three Axes of Parallelism

Data parallelSplit the batch
Every GPU holds a full copy of the model, processes different samples, then averages gradients. Attacks the compute wall.
Tensor (intra-layer)Split each layer
Partition the weight matrices of a single layer across GPUs. Attacks the per-layer memory & compute wall. [Shoeybi et al. 2019]
Pipeline (inter-layer)Split by depth
Different GPUs own different layers; activations flow stage-to-stage. Attacks the whole-model memory wall. [Huang et al. 2019]
Vocabulary Data parallelism replicates and communicates gradients; model parallelism (tensor + pipeline) partitions the model and communicates activations. The largest systems combine all three — 3D parallelism (Module 6.4). [Narayanan et al. 2021]

Data Parallelism: The Basic Loop

Idea Replicate the full model on \(N\) workers. Split each mini-batch into \(N\) shards; every worker does a local forward/backward on its shard, producing a local gradient.
The one synchronization Before the optimizer step, workers must agree on the average gradient \( \bar g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i g_i \). This is an all-reduce — the collective from our message-passing module — over the model's entire gradient vector, every step.
Why it works Averaging gradients over \(N\) shards is mathematically identical to one large batch of size \(N\times\) the shard — the same SGD, just faster. Communication, not correctness, is the cost.
Original figure; standard data-parallel synchronous SGD.

Parameter Server vs. All-Reduce

Parameter server Workers push grads to / pull params from central server(s). Simple, but server bandwidth scales as \(O(N)\) → a hot spot as \(N\) grows.
All-reduce (decentralized) No server: workers exchange with neighbors. Cost is independent of \(N\) with the right algorithm — see ring all-reduce next. [Gibiansky 2017]

Ring All-Reduce (Bandwidth-Optimal)

Mechanism Arrange \(N\) GPUs in a logical ring; chunk each gradient vector into \(N\) pieces. Run two phases: a scatter-reduce (each GPU ends owning the full sum of one chunk), then an all-gather (broadcast those summed chunks around the ring). [Gibiansky 2017]

Each phase is \(N-1\) steps; per step a GPU sends \( \tfrac{K}{N} \) bytes:

$$ T_{\text{ring}} \;=\; 2\,(N-1)\cdot\frac{K}{N\,B} \;\xrightarrow{\;N\text{ large}\;}\; \frac{2K}{B} $$

\(K\)=gradient bytes, \(B\)=link bandwidth. The data each GPU moves is ~2K regardless of \(N\) — that is why ring all-reduce scales.

Horovod Uber's Horovod brought ring all-reduce to mainstream frameworks with a near drop-in API, plus tensor fusion (batch small tensors into one all-reduce to amortize latency). [Sergeev & Del Balso 2018]
In hardware NVIDIA's NCCL implements ring (and tree) all-reduce over NVLink/InfiniBand; it is the collective under PyTorch DDP. [NVIDIA NCCL docs; PyTorch DDP docs]
Connects to This is the same all-reduce collective from message passing — here applied to gradient vectors, and made topology-aware for the interconnect.

ZeRO: Removing Data-Parallel Redundancy

The waste Plain data parallelism stores a full copy of params, gradients, and optimizer states on every GPU — for \(N\) GPUs that is \(N\times\) redundant state.
ZeRO idea Partition the model states across the \(N\) data-parallel GPUs instead of replicating; gather just-in-time via collectives. Three cumulative stages. [Rajbhandari et al. 2020]
  • Stage 1 (Pos): shard optimizer states → ~4× less
  • Stage 2 (+grad): also shard gradients → ~8× less
  • Stage 3 (+param): also shard parameters → linear in \(N\)
ConfigMemory / GPU*
Baseline DP\(16\Psi\)
Stage 1\(4\Psi + \tfrac{12\Psi}{N}\)
Stage 2\(2\Psi + \tfrac{14\Psi}{N}\)
Stage 3\(\tfrac{16\Psi}{N}\)

*\(\Psi\)=#params; bytes-per-param model from [Rajbhandari et al. 2020]. Example: a 7.5B model at Stage 3 on 64 GPUs needs ~1.9 GB of state each vs. ~120 GB replicated.

Key move ZeRO keeps the simplicity of data parallelism (no model surgery) but pays a modest extra communication cost to eliminate the memory redundancy — enabling trillion-parameter training on commodity DP.

FSDP & Activation Checkpointing

PyTorch FSDP Fully Sharded Data Parallel is PyTorch's native realization of ZeRO-3: each unit's parameters live sharded; an all-gather reconstructs them just before a layer runs, they are freed after, and a reduce-scatter shards the gradients. [Zhao et al. 2023]
Why it matters Turns ZeRO's ideas into an industry-grade, co-designed API — the default way large models are trained in PyTorch today.
Activation checkpointing (re-materialization) Activations can dwarf parameter memory. Don't store every layer's activations — keep a few checkpoints and recompute the rest during the backward pass.

Trades memory for compute: activation memory drops from \(O(L)\) toward \(O(\sqrt{L})\) at the cost of one extra forward pass (~⅓ more compute). First used at scale in GPipe. [Huang et al. 2019]

Together Sharding (ZeRO/FSDP) attacks parameter/optimizer memory; checkpointing attacks activation memory. They are orthogonal and routinely combined.

Module 6.1 — Review

1. In data parallelism, what is communicated each step, and by which collective?

The averaged gradient over all replicas, via an all-reduce. Weights stay in sync because every worker applies the same averaged gradient.

2. Why is ring all-reduce preferred over a parameter server at scale?

A parameter server's bandwidth is an \(O(N)\) hot spot. Ring all-reduce is decentralized and moves ~2K bytes per GPU regardless of \(N\) — bandwidth-optimal [Gibiansky 2017].

3. What redundancy does ZeRO/FSDP remove, and how far can Stage 3 go?

Data parallelism replicates params, gradients and optimizer states on every GPU. ZeRO partitions them; Stage 3 makes per-GPU state linear in \(N\) (\(16\Psi/N\)) [Rajbhandari et al. 2020].

Model Parallelism & Device Placement

Naive model parallelism Assign different parts of one model to different devices when it will not fit on one. But which op goes where? Bad placement leaves devices idle and adds cross-device traffic.
Learned placement (ColocRL) Mirhoseini et al. framed placement as reinforcement learning: a sequence-to-sequence policy assigns TensorFlow graph ops to devices, using measured runtime as the reward (REINFORCE). It found placements faster than human experts for Inception-v3, RNN LMs and NMT. [Mirhoseini et al. 2017]
The limitation Placement alone still runs devices one after another — the same idle-time (bubble) problem pipeline parallelism will fix (Module 6.3).
Two flavors of model parallelism
  • Tensor (intra-layer): split within a layer — this module
  • Pipeline (inter-layer): split across layers — next module

Megatron: Tensor-Parallel MLP

The block A Transformer MLP is \( Y=\text{GeLU}(XA)\), \(Z=YB\). Megatron splits weight \(A\) by columns and \(B\) by rows so the nonlinearity needs no sync. [Shoeybi et al. 2019]

Column-split \(A=[A_1,A_2]\): each GPU computes its own \(\text{GeLU}(XA_i)\) independently (GeLU is elementwise).

Row-split \(B=[B_1;B_2]\): partial products \(Y_iB_i\) are summed by one all-reduce (the \(g\) operator) to form \(Z\).

Column-parallel = "partition the output"; row-parallel = "reduce the output". Chaining them confines communication to a single all-reduce per block.

Adapted from Fig. 3 of [Shoeybi et al. 2019].

Megatron: Attention, and the f / g Operators

Self-attention splits naturally Multi-head attention is parallel across heads: give each GPU a subset of heads (column-parallel on \(Q,K,V\)), then the output projection is row-parallel — one all-reduce, exactly like the MLP. [Shoeybi et al. 2019]
Conjugate operators f and g
  • f: identity in forward; all-reduce in backward
  • g: all-reduce in forward; identity in backward
Each attention block and each MLP block has one \(f\) and one \(g\).

Communication per Transformer layer:

$$ \underbrace{2}_{\text{fwd: }g\text{ in attn}+ g\text{ in MLP}} + \underbrace{2}_{\text{bwd: }f\text{ in attn}+ f\text{ in MLP}} = 4 \text{ all-reduces} $$

Each all-reduce is over the tensor-parallel group, on an activation tensor of size batch×seq×hidden. [Shoeybi et al. 2019]

The cost These all-reduces sit on the critical path and are frequent → tensor parallelism is kept within a high-bandwidth node (NVLink), not across slow network links.
Result Megatron trained an 8.3B-parameter model on 512 GPUs at 76% scaling efficiency (15.1 PetaFLOP/s sustained) with only a few communication ops added to native PyTorch. [Shoeybi et al. 2019]

Module 6.2 — Review

1. Why split \(A\) by columns and \(B\) by rows in the Megatron MLP?

Column-splitting \(A\) lets each GPU apply the elementwise GeLU to its own slice with no synchronization; row-splitting \(B\) means the partial outputs need just one all-reduce to combine. [Shoeybi et al. 2019]

2. What are the f and g operators, and how many all-reduces per layer?

\(g\) all-reduces in the forward pass, \(f\) all-reduces in the backward pass. With one \(f\)+\(g\) in attention and one in the MLP, a layer costs 4 all-reduces per iteration.

3. Why is tensor parallelism confined to a single node?

Its all-reduces are on the critical path every layer, so it demands the highest-bandwidth links (NVLink within a DGX node); crossing slow network links would stall the GPUs.

Splitting by Depth — and the Bubble

Pipeline parallelism Assign contiguous layers to \(p\) devices ("stages"). Stage \(k\) sends its activations to stage \(k{+}1\). But run one mini-batch at a time and only one stage is busy at any moment — the rest are idle. That idle time is the pipeline bubble.
GPipe's fix: micro-batches Split the mini-batch into \(m\) micro-batches and feed them in back-to-back, so multiple stages work concurrently — exactly like an instruction pipeline filling up. [Huang et al. 2019]

GPipe also uses re-materialization (activation checkpointing) so memory does not grow with \(m\), and keeps gradients numerically identical to serial training.

Adapted from the GPipe schedule, Fig. 2 of [Huang et al. 2019].

Bubble Math: Worked Numbers

With \(p\) stages and \(m\) micro-batches, the pipeline fills and drains over \(p-1\) steps. The bubble fraction of total time is:

$$ \text{bubble} \;=\; \frac{p-1}{m+p-1}\;\approx\;\frac{p-1}{m} $$

Fill/drain idle steps divided by useful steps. [Huang et al. 2019], [Narayanan et al. 2021].

Rule of thumb: keep \(m \gtrsim 4p\) so the bubble is small — GPipe notes overhead is negligible once \(m \ge 4p\). [Huang et al. 2019]

Tension Bigger \(m\) shrinks the bubble but increases in-flight activation memory (mitigated by checkpointing) and needs a large enough global batch. This trade-off is what 1F1B and interleaving optimize next.

1F1B: PipeDream's Steady State

GPipe's weakness GPipe does all forwards, then all backwards → activations for every in-flight micro-batch pile up, so peak activation memory grows with \(m\).
1F1B (one-forward-one-backward) PipeDream interleaves: once the pipeline is full, each stage strictly alternates one forward, one backward. A micro-batch's activations are freed as soon as its backward runs → peak memory bounded by \(\approx p\), not \(m\). [Narayanan et al. 2019]

Same asymptotic bubble as GPipe, but far lower memory — PipeDream reports up to 5.3× faster training than prior intra-batch methods. Weight stashing/versioning keeps gradients consistent. [Narayanan et al. 2019]

Adapted from the 1F1B schedule in [Narayanan et al. 2019] / [Narayanan et al. 2021].

Interleaved 1F1B (Megatron-LM)

Idea Give each device \(v\) non-contiguous "model chunks" instead of one contiguous block of layers. Each device is revisited \(v\) times per pass, so the fill/drain overhead is divided by \(v\). [Narayanan et al. 2021]
$$ \text{bubble}_{\text{interleaved}} \;=\; \frac{1}{v}\cdot\frac{p-1}{m} $$

Smaller bubble at the cost of more communication (each chunk boundary is a point-to-point send). [Narayanan et al. 2021]

Payoff Interleaving improved throughput by more than 10% at scale, and combined with tensor + data parallelism enabled a 1-trillion-parameter configuration at 502 PetaFLOP/s on 3072 GPUs. [Narayanan et al. 2021]
Pattern GPipe → 1F1B → interleaved 1F1B is a steady march: same exact-gradient math, progressively less bubble and less memory, paid for with more scheduling and communication complexity.

Module 6.3 — Review

1. What is the pipeline bubble, and how do micro-batches shrink it?

Idle stage time while the pipeline fills/drains. Splitting into \(m\) micro-batches drives the fraction to \(\frac{p-1}{m+p-1}\); keep \(m\gtrsim 4p\) to make it small [Huang et al. 2019].

2. What problem does 1F1B solve versus GPipe?

GPipe holds activations for all \(m\) in-flight micro-batches. 1F1B alternates forward/backward so activations are freed early — peak memory bounded by \(\approx p\), not \(m\) [Narayanan et al. 2019].

3. How does interleaving reduce the bubble, and at what cost?

Assigning \(v\) model chunks per device divides the bubble by \(v\) (\(\frac{1}{v}\frac{p-1}{m}\)), at the cost of more point-to-point communication [Narayanan et al. 2021].

3D (Hybrid) Parallelism

Compose all three
  • Tensor — within a node (NVLink), splits each layer
  • Pipeline — across nodes, splits by depth
  • Data — replicates the whole pipeline, all-reduces gradients
Total GPUs \(= t \times p \times d\). Map the most communication-heavy dimension (tensor) to the fastest links. [Narayanan et al. 2021]
Why hybrid No single axis scales alone: tensor is node-bound, pipeline adds bubble, data replicates memory. Combining them (plus ZeRO) is how frontier models are trained.

Aside: Mixture-of-Experts (Scaling Params, Not FLOPs)

Sparsely-gated MoE Replace a dense layer with many expert sub-networks and a gate that routes each token to only a few experts. Parameters grow hugely while per-token compute stays roughly constant.
GShard Scaled a translation Transformer past 600B parameters on 2048 TPU v3 in 4 days with top-2 routing and automatic sharding. [Lepikhin et al. 2020]
Switch Transformer Simplified routing to a single expert (top-1), reaching trillion-parameter scale with lower communication and better stability. [Fedus et al. 2021]
New systems cost Routing scatters tokens across experts on different GPUs → an all-to-all collective becomes the bottleneck (a new axis: expert parallelism).
Why it matters here MoE is the "1T+" bar from slide 1: it decouples parameter count from compute, but adds an all-to-all communication pattern on top of the 3D-parallel stack.

More Axes: Expert, Sequence & Context Parallelism

Beyond the three core axes (data · tensor · pipeline), three more appear once models go sparse (MoE) or contexts get very long.

Expert parallelism (EP) For MoE: shard the experts across GPUs and route each token to its assigned experts through an all-to-all. Scales parameters, not FLOPs. [Lepikhin et al. 2020; Fedus et al. 2021]
Sequence parallelism (SP) Split activations along the sequence dimension for the LayerNorm / Dropout regions tensor-parallelism leaves replicated → cuts activation memory, no extra compute. [Korthikanti et al. 2022]
Context parallelism (CP) Split all activations (including attention) along the sequence dimension, using ring attention to pass K/V between GPUs → trains very long contexts. Broader than SP. [Liu et al. 2023]
Putting it together Large-scale MoE + long-context training now stacks up to 5 axes: data · tensor · pipeline · expert · context (with sequence parallelism as a memory-saving companion to tensor parallelism).

Topic 6 — Synthesis Review

1. A model fits on one GPU but training is too slow. Which parallelism, and what is communicated?

Data parallelism: replicate the model, split the batch, and all-reduce gradients each step (ring all-reduce for scalability).

2. A single layer's weights won't fit on one GPU. Which parallelism, and what's the communication cost?

Tensor parallelism (Megatron): column-then-row split; 4 all-reduces per layer, so keep it within a high-bandwidth node [Shoeybi et al. 2019].

3. The whole model won't fit even sharded per layer. What do you add, and what's the new overhead?

Pipeline parallelism across depth — introducing the bubble \(\frac{p-1}{m+p-1}\), reduced by micro-batches, 1F1B, and interleaving; then wrap it all in data parallelism for 3D parallelism.
